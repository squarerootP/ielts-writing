# Tips from Codex

# 1. The core principle

For Task 2, do **not** think:

> “What sophisticated phrase can I use here?”
> 

Think:

> “What logical job does this sentence need to perform?”
> 

Typical jobs are:

- state a position;
- concede part of the opposing view;
- introduce a reason;
- explain a mechanism;
- give an example;
- qualify a claim;
- compare two sides;
- show consequence;
- weigh importance;
- conclude.

Your goal is to make these functions automatic.

---

# 2. Introductions: paraphrase + position

A Task 2 introduction normally needs only two things:

1. what the debate/problem is;
2. your position.

You usually do **not** need a long background statement.

## Useful opening patterns

| Function | Natural wording | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Introduce disagreement | **Opinions differ over whether...** | *Opinions differ over whether scientific research should focus primarily on health problems.* |
| Neutral debate | **There is ongoing debate over whether...** | *There is ongoing debate over whether university education should be free.* |
| State a trend | **It is increasingly common for...** | *It is increasingly common for young adults to work abroad.* |
| Introduce two views | **Some argue that..., whereas others believe that...** | *Some argue that charities should operate locally, whereas others believe they should provide aid internationally.* |
| Introduce a problem | **X has become an increasingly important issue in many countries.** | *Housing affordability has become an increasingly important issue in many cities.* |

## Position language

### Strong agreement

> **I completely agree that...**
> 

> **I strongly agree with this view because...**
> 

### Partial agreement

> **I largely agree, although...**
> 

> **While I accept that X has certain benefits, I believe Y is more significant.**
> 

### Disagreement

> **I do not believe that this is necessarily the case.**
> 

> **I disagree with this view because...**
> 

### Conditional / nuanced position

> **In my view, the most appropriate approach depends largely on...**
> 

> **Whether this is beneficial depends on...**
> 

These are extremely useful because not every question needs a binary answer.

---

# 3. Avoid accidentally changing the proposition

This is one of the most important Task Response lessons from the examiner feedback we have been reviewing.

If the prompt says:

> hard work **can** lead to success
> 

do not silently change it into:

> hard work is the **only** factor in success.
> 

Similarly:

| Prompt word | Exact force |
| --- | --- |
| **can** | possibility |
| **always** | universality |
| **mainly** | primary cause |
| **only** | exclusivity |
| **should** | recommendation |
| **more important** | comparison |
| **outweigh** | relative judgment |
| **to what extent** | degree of agreement |

Before writing, ask:

> **What exactly am I being asked to prove?**
> 

This is the Task 2 equivalent of checking the graph before choosing *rose steadily* in Task 1.

# 4. Topic sentences

A strong topic sentence should directly answer the question.

## Useful patterns

### Advantages

> **One major advantage of X is that...**
> 

> **A significant benefit of X is...**
> 

> **X can be particularly beneficial because...**
> 

### Disadvantages

> **One major drawback is that...**
> 

> **A more serious concern is...**
> 

> **X can also create substantial problems for...**
> 

### Causes

> **One important reason for this trend is...**
> 

> **This can largely be explained by...**
> 

> **A major factor contributing to this problem is...**
> 

### Solutions

> **One effective way to address this problem is to...**
> 

> **A practical measure would be to...**
> 

> **Governments could tackle this issue by...**
> 

### Discuss both views

> **Supporters of this view argue that...**
> 

> **Those who oppose this approach may be concerned that...**
> 

> **On the other hand, proponents of X argue that...**
> 

### Your preferred side

> **Nevertheless, I believe the stronger argument is that...**
> 

> **Despite these concerns, I believe...**
>

# 5. Explaining a mechanism

This is where many Band 6–7 essays become weak.

Do not merely write:

> X is beneficial.
> 

Explain:

> X → how it works → result.
> 

## Useful causal patterns

| Function | Phrase |
| --- | --- |
| Direct cause | **because...** |
| Result | **As a result,...** |
| Chain reaction | **This, in turn,...** |
| Consequence | **Consequently,...** |
| Mechanism | **By doing so,...** |
| Condition | **If this occurs,...** |
| Explanation | **This is because...** |
| Broader effect | **This can ultimately lead to...** |

Example:

> International scientific collaboration allows researchers to share data and expertise. **This, in turn, reduces unnecessary duplication of research and enables discoveries to be made more quickly.**
> 

That is much better than:

> International cooperation is good because scientists can work together.
> 

---

# 6. Cause-and-effect chains

A very useful Task 2 habit is to think in chains of 2–4 steps.

### Example: overwork

> longer working hours
> 
> 
> → less leisure and sleep
> 
> → chronic stress and burnout
> 
> → poorer mental health and family relationships
> 

Then write:

> Long working hours reduce the amount of time available for sleep and leisure. **As a result**, employees may experience chronic stress and burnout, which can ultimately damage both their mental health and family relationships.
> 

### Example: museums

> outdated exhibits
> 
> 
> → locals perceive museums as irrelevant
> 
> → little incentive to revisit
> 
> → low local attendance
> 

### Example: scientific cooperation

> shared expertise
> 
> 
> → less duplicated effort
> 
> → faster research
> 
> → quicker solutions to global problems
> 

This is Task 2’s version of recognizing a visual trend in Task 1.

---

# 7. Giving examples

Examples should prove the **specific claim**, not merely something generally related.

## Safe patterns

> **For example,...**
> 

> **For instance,...**
> 

> **A clear example of this is...**
> 

> **This can be seen in...**
> 

## More important principle

Your example should satisfy:

> **claim → example → why the example proves the claim**
> 

Example:

> Health research remains essential because some diseases continue to pose serious challenges. **For example, antibiotic resistance is making certain infections increasingly difficult to treat, which means continued medical research is necessary to develop new therapies.**
> 

That is better than:

> COVID vaccines were successful.
> 

because the latter proves past achievement, not necessarily a current research priority.

---

# 8. Avoid fake evidence language

Be cautious with:

> *Recent statistics indicate that...*
> 

> *Studies have proven that...*
> 

> *Scientific evidence shows that...*
> 

unless you actually know what you are referring to.

IELTS does not require citations.

Safer:

> **Many young adults may have limited experience managing debt...**
> 

> **Long working hours can contribute to stress and burnout...**
> 

> **People from disadvantaged backgrounds may have fewer educational opportunities...**
> 

Measured claims are usually more persuasive than unsupported authority.

---

# 9. Qualification: extremely useful for Band 8

Avoid making claims stronger than your argument can support.

## Useful qualifiers

| Too strong | Better |
| --- | --- |
| all | many / some |
| always | often / frequently |
| causes | can contribute to |
| guarantees | increases the likelihood of |
| prevents | reduces |
| proves | suggests / illustrates |
| everyone | a large proportion of people |
| impossible | considerably more difficult |

Example:

> Homeschooling **causes** poor social skills. ❌
> 

Better:

> Homeschooling **may reduce opportunities for regular peer interaction**, particularly when children have limited access to extracurricular activities. ✅
> 

This is much safer academically.

---

# 10. Concession language

A concession lets you acknowledge a valid opposing point without abandoning your position.

## Useful patterns

> **Admittedly,...**
> 

> **Granted,...**
> 

> **While it is true that..., ...**
> 

> **Although X can..., Y remains...**
> 

> **Despite these benefits,...**
> 

Example:

> **Granted**, working longer hours can increase total economic output in some industries. **However**, excessive working time can eventually reduce efficiency through fatigue.
> 

This is especially useful for:

- agree/disagree;
- advantages/disadvantages;
- discuss both views.

---

# 11. Contrast

| Function | Useful language |
| --- | --- |
| Basic contrast | **However,...** |
| Paragraph contrast | **On the other hand,...** |
| Direct comparison | **Whereas...** |
| Concession | **Although / While...** |
| Stronger counterpoint | **Nevertheless,...** |
| Refer back | **Despite these benefits / concerns,...** |

Avoid unnecessarily formal alternatives just for variety.

For example:

> **Despite these benefits** ✅
> 

is usually preferable to:

> **Notwithstanding the aforementioned advantages** ⚠️
> 

---

# 12. Adding a second reason

You do not need fifteen synonyms for *Furthermore*.

Useful options:

> **In addition,...**
> 

> **Furthermore,...**
> 

> **Moreover,...**
> 

> **Another important factor is...**
> 

> **A further concern is...**
> 

> **Another reason is that...**
> 

Often the noun phrase is better than the linker:

> **A further difficulty is that international projects are harder to coordinate.**
> 

This gives the reader more information than merely:

> Furthermore,...
> 

---

# 13. Referring back to previous ideas

Good cohesion often comes from reference words rather than obvious linking phrases.

Useful expressions:

> **this problem**
> 

> **these difficulties**
> 

> **such measures**
> 

> **these benefits**
> 

> **this approach**
> 

> **such organizations**
> 

> **these factors**
> 

> **this trend**
> 

Example:

> International projects can create confidentiality and coordination problems. **These difficulties**, however, are outweighed by the benefits of knowledge sharing.
> 

This is strong cohesion without sounding mechanical.

---

# 14. Weighing arguments

Especially important for **outweigh** questions.

Do not merely write:

> the disadvantages outweigh the advantages.
> 

Explain **why**.

Useful criteria:

- number of people affected;
- severity;
- duration;
- reversibility;
- scale;
- long-term consequences;
- breadth of impact;
- availability of alternatives.

## Useful patterns

> **The more significant issue is that...**
> 

> **More importantly,...**
> 

> **This drawback is particularly serious because...**
> 

> **The benefits are relatively limited compared with...**
> 

> **X affects a much broader section of society than Y.**
> 

> **The long-term consequences are likely to be more substantial.**
> 

Example:

> Community gardens benefit residents who are interested in cultivation, whereas public parks serve children, adults and elderly people alike. **For this reason, the loss of flexible recreational space may affect a much broader section of the community.**
> 

That is genuine weighing.

---

# 15. Agree/disagree language

For **To what extent do you agree or disagree?**

## Full agreement

> **I completely agree with this view for two main reasons.**
> 

## Largely agree

> **I largely agree, although the relationship is not absolute.**
> 

## Partial agreement

> **I agree that X is important, but I do not believe it is sufficient on its own.**
> 

## Disagreement

> **I disagree with this claim because it overlooks...**
> 

But remember the proposition itself.

If the statement says:

> X **can** cause Y
> 

it is harder to disagree than if it says:

> X **always** causes Y.
>

# 16. Advantages/disadvantages language

## Introducing benefits

> **One major benefit is...**
> 

> **X provides individuals with...**
> 

> **X can enable people to...**
> 

> **X offers greater access to...**
> 

## Introducing drawbacks

> **A major drawback is...**
> 

> **X can place people at risk of...**
> 

> **X may reduce opportunities for...**
> 

> **X can create considerable pressure on...**
> 

## Weighing

> **Nevertheless, I believe the disadvantages are more substantial because...**
> 

> **Although these benefits are meaningful, they are outweighed by...**
> 

Avoid:

> **perceived benefits**
> 

unless you truly mean that the benefits are not real.

# 17. Problem–solution language

## Problems

> **One major cause of this problem is...**
> 

> **This issue is largely driven by...**
> 

> **Another contributing factor is...**
> 

## Solutions

> **One effective measure would be to...**
> 

> **Governments could address this by...**
> 

> **A longer-term solution would involve...**
> 

> **This would help to...**
> 

## Connecting solution to problem

This is crucial:

> problem → targeted intervention → expected result
> 

Example:

> Museums may appear irrelevant because exhibitions remain unchanged for years. **One effective solution would be to introduce temporary exhibitions and interactive displays, giving local residents a reason to return.**
> 

That is much stronger than:

> Governments should improve museums.
> 

---

# 18. Discuss-both-views essays

A very reliable structure is:

### Introduction

paraphrase + your opinion

### Body 1

why View A is reasonable

### Body 2

why View B is reasonable + why you prefer it

### Conclusion

restate position

Useful transitions:

> **On the one hand,...**
> 

> **Supporters of this view argue that...**
> 

> **On the other hand,...**
> 

> **Despite these concerns, I believe...**
> 

You do not need a separate third body paragraph for your opinion unless there is a strong reason.

---

# 19. Two-part questions

If the prompt asks:

> Why is this the case?
> 
> 
> What can be done?
> 

A clean structure is:

### Body 1

causes

### Body 2

solutions

Likewise:

> What problems does this cause?
> 
> 
> How can they be solved?
> 

Do not mix everything randomly.

This makes Task Response extremely easy for the examiner to follow.

# 22. Common lexical traps we have already encountered

### propensity

Avoid using **propensity** to mean *trend*.

> this propensity is negative ❌
> 

> this trend is negative ✅
> 

### concomitant with

Not wrong, but often needlessly heavy.

> benefits concomitant with credit-card ownership ⚠️
> 

> benefits of credit-card ownership ✅
> 

### imperil

Possible, but often overly dramatic.

> imperil young individuals ⚠️
> 

> place young adults at financial risk ✅
> 

### citizenry

Usually unnecessary.

> the general citizenry ⚠️
> 

> local residents / the public ✅
> 

### facilitate

Be careful with grammar:

> facilitate them to assist ❌
> 

> enable them to assist ✅
> 

> facilitate assistance ✅
> 

### accustomed

> accustomed to local history ❌ if you mean knowledgeable
> 

> familiar with local history ✅
> 

### enhance

> enhance quality of life ✅
> 

This is valid, though **improve quality of life** is more common.

# 23. Useful sentence patterns for Band 8

These are worth making automatic.

### Cause → result

> **Because X..., Y...**
> 

> **X can lead to Y, which in turn...**
> 

### Concession

> **Although X can provide..., it may also...**
> 

### Conditional

> **If this problem is not addressed, it may...**
> 

### Relative clause

> **People who lack access to... may...**
> 

### By + gerund

> **By sharing expertise, scientists can reduce duplicated effort.**
> 

### Not only... but also...

> **This not only improves efficiency but also reduces costs.**
> 

Use it occasionally, not everywhere.

### Whereas

> **Local charities may lack resources, whereas international organizations can operate across borders.**

# 24. Useful ways to avoid repetition

Suppose your topic is *charities*.

You can rotate naturally among:

> charities
> 
> 
> charitable organizations
> 
> these organizations
> 
> local charities
> 
> international charities
> 

But do **not** force strange synonyms just to avoid repetition.

Likewise for:

> people → individuals → residents → workers
> 

Only use the alternative if it accurately refers to the same group.

Repetition is often preferable to semantic distortion.

# 25. Conclusions

A conclusion should:

- restate your position;
- summarize the main logic;
- introduce no new idea.

## Reliable patterns

> **In conclusion, although X..., I believe Y...**
> 

> **Overall, X remains important, but Y should receive greater attention because...**
> 

> **In conclusion, the most effective approach is to...**
> 

Avoid generic recommendations unless the question invites them.

For example:

> Individuals should carefully consider their options.
> 

often adds little.

>

> 

> **tends to**
> 

> **can contribute to**
> 

> **is more likely to**
> 

rather than:

> always
> 
> 
> inevitably
> 
> proves
> 
> guarantees
> 
> eliminates
> 

unless the logic truly supports the absolute claim.

# 27. One useful test for every body paragraph

After drafting a paragraph, ask:

### 1. What is my main claim?

Can you state it in one sentence?

### 2. How does it answer the exact question?

Not merely the topic.

### 3. Have I explained the mechanism?

Why/how does the effect occur?

### 4. Does my example prove this exact claim?

Not something adjacent.

### 5. Have I overstated anything?

Could *causes* become *can contribute to*?

That five-question check is extremely useful.

# 28. A practical paragraph formula

You do not have to mechanically follow this, but it is a good default:

> **Topic sentence**
> 
> 
> → explanation
> 
> → consequence/mechanism
> 
> → example
> 
> → link back to argument
> 

Example:

> One major disadvantage of excessive working hours is their effect on family life. Employees who regularly remain at work late have less time to spend with their partners and children. **As a result**, relationships may become strained, while some young adults may postpone marriage or parenthood. This can contribute to lower birth rates in societies where long working hours are widespread. **For this reason**, the social cost of overwork can extend beyond individual employees.
> 

That is a complete paragraph without difficult vocabulary.

# 30. The Task 2 equivalent of your Task 1 “pattern mapping”

For Task 1, the goal was:

> irregular but downward → **broadly declining**
> 

> rise then flat → **levelled off**
> 

> crossing lines → **overtook**
> 

For Task 2, I want you to reach the same automaticity:

> acknowledge opposite side → **Granted / Although...**
> 

> cause produces another effect → **This, in turn,...**
> 

> claim is not universal → **may / can / tends to**
> 

> second argument is stronger → **More importantly,...**
> 

> first view valid but weaker → **Despite these benefits,...**
> 

> problem → consequence → **As a result / Consequently**
> 

> policy → intended outcome → **This would help to...**
> 

> broader impact → **affects several aspects of society at once**
> 

> avoid absolute claim → **can contribute to** rather than **causes**
> 

That is probably the most useful way to train Task 2: **logical pattern → linguistic pattern**, just as your Task 1 notes trained **visual pattern → linguistic pattern**.
