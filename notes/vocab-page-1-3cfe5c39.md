# Vocab Page 1

| Word | IPA | Refined Definition | Example | Register | Common Collocations | IELTS Usefulness | Similar words & precise usage boundaries |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **peculiar** | /pɪˈkjuːliə/ | Strange, unusual, or distinctive | *Context managers are quite a peculiar feature that differentiates Python.* | Neutral | peculiar habit, peculiar feature | Good for describing uniqueness | **Similar:** unusual, strange, distinctive, odd. **Use peculiar** when something noticeably departs from what is normal or has a characteristic uniquely associated with it; it can suggest strangeness without necessarily being negative. *A peculiar feature of the system* = an unusual/distinctive feature. **Use unusual** for the safest neutral statement of rarity. **Strange/odd** when you explicitly mean weird, puzzling, or unexpected. **Distinctive** when the important point is that something makes an entity recognizable, not that it is strange. |
| **salvage** | /ˈsælvɪdʒ/ | To save or rescue something from loss or failure | *We could try to salvage the situation.* | Formal | salvage reputation, salvage operation | Useful in problem-solving essays | **Similar:** save, rescue, recover, retrieve. **Use salvage** when something is already damaged, endangered, or close to failure and you manage to preserve at least part of its value: *salvage a reputation, relationship, project, situation*. **Save** is much broader and does not imply prior damage. **Rescue** normally concerns people/animals or something being freed from immediate danger. **Recover** means regain something lost or return to a previous state; you can *recover lost data* but normally not *salvage your health*. |
| **pragmatic** | /præɡˈmætɪk/ | Practical, focused on workable solutions rather than theory | *We need a pragmatic solution—something that works now.* | Formal | pragmatic approach, pragmatic decision | Excellent for Task 2 essays | **Similar:** practical, realistic, sensible, utilitarian. **Use pragmatic** when a decision prioritizes what will actually work, sometimes accepting compromises instead of following ideals or theory. **Practical** is broader and can simply mean useful or feasible. **Realistic** emphasizes accurate recognition of circumstances or limitations. **Sensible** emphasizes good judgment. A person can wear *practical shoes*, but calling the shoes *pragmatic* would normally be wrong because pragmatism concerns reasoning or policy rather than physical usefulness. |
| **insurmountable** | /ˌɪnsəˈmaʊntəbl/ | Too great or difficult to overcome | *The team faced an insurmountable challenge after their funding was cut.* | Formal | insurmountable obstacle, insurmountable challenge, insurmountable difficulties | Strong for describing major challenges | **Similar:** impossible, overwhelming, formidable, prohibitive. **Use insurmountable** specifically for an **obstacle, difficulty, barrier, problem, or challenge that cannot be overcome**. **Formidable** means extremely difficult but still potentially conquerable. **Overwhelming** means too powerful or intense to handle and can describe feelings, evidence, numbers, etc. **Impossible** is broader: an *impossible deadline* works, whereas an *insurmountable deadline* is unnatural; the obstacle created by the deadline may be insurmountable. **Prohibitive** usually means so high/costly/difficult that it prevents action, especially *prohibitive costs*. |
| **jeopardize** | /ˈdʒepədaɪz/ | To put something at risk or in danger | *A devaluation of the dollar would jeopardize New York's position.* | Formal | jeopardize future, jeopardize success, jeopardize safety | Useful in economic/policy contexts | **Similar:** endanger, threaten, risk, compromise. **Use jeopardize** when an action or circumstance makes a desirable outcome, position, relationship, safety, career, etc. liable to be lost or damaged. **Endanger** is strongest for physical safety, lives, species, or existence. **Threaten** can describe either creating danger or explicitly making a threat. **Risk** can mean voluntarily expose something to danger: *He risked his career*. **Compromise** is especially good when integrity, security, effectiveness, anonymity, or standards are weakened rather than completely lost. |
| **diabolical** | /ˌdaɪəˈbɒlɪkl/ | Extremely bad, unpleasant, or cruel | *A singer with an absolutely diabolical voice.* | Informal/Strong | diabolical plan, diabolical weather, diabolical conditions | Less suitable for formal IELTS writing | **Similar:** terrible, atrocious, appalling, wicked, evil. **Use diabolical** either for something extremely bad in an emphatic, often humorous British sense (*diabolical service/weather*) or literally for something extremely evil/cunning (*a diabolical scheme*). **Terrible** is the ordinary broad alternative. **Appalling/atrocious** are stronger but more conventional in formal criticism. **Evil/wicked** should be used when moral wrongdoing is genuinely intended; *diabolical traffic* does not mean morally evil traffic. |
| **mnemonic** | /nɪˈmɒnɪk/ | A device, pattern, or technique that helps someone remember information | *Acronyms can serve as mnemonic devices.* | Formal/Academic | mnemonic device, mnemonic technique, mnemonic aid | Useful in education and learning contexts | **Similar:** memory aid, reminder, memorisation technique. **Use mnemonic** for a deliberately constructed device or pattern that encodes information so that it is easier to remember: acronyms, rhymes, associations, etc. **Memory aid** is the broader everyday term and can include notes, alarms, or physical prompts. **Reminder** merely prompts you to remember something; a calendar notification is a reminder but normally not a mnemonic. |
| **taxonomy** | /tækˈsɒnəmi/ | A system or method of classifying things into groups | *The taxonomy of these fossils has been revised several times.* | Formal/Academic | taxonomy of species, classification taxonomy, hierarchical taxonomy | Useful in scientific/academic contexts | **Similar:** classification, categorisation, typology, hierarchy. **Use taxonomy** for an organized **system of categories**, usually governed by explicit principles and often hierarchical. **Classification** can refer either to the process of assigning things to classes or the resulting grouping and is much broader. **Categorisation** focuses on the act of grouping. **Typology** classifies things into types based on shared characteristics, often for analytical purposes, without necessarily forming a hierarchy. A one-off division of survey respondents by age is usually a *classification/categorisation*, not necessarily a *taxonomy*. |
| **utterly** | /ˈʌtəli/ | Completely or absolutely, usually used for emphasis | *Such an approach would be utterly ineffective.* | Neutral | utterly useless, utterly wrong, utterly unacceptable | Strong intensifier; use selectively in essays | **Similar:** completely, absolutely, entirely, totally. **Use utterly** primarily with adjectives expressing an extreme or categorical state, often negative: *utterly unacceptable, utterly absurd, utterly impossible*. It has stronger rhetorical force than **completely/entirely**. **Completely** is the safest general intensifier. **Absolutely** works particularly with non-gradable/extreme adjectives: *absolutely essential*. **Entirely** is formal and restrained. Avoid combinations such as *utterly slightly different* because *utterly* requires completeness/extremity. |
| **peril** | /ˈperəl/ | Serious or immediate danger | *A failure to address climate change could place vulnerable communities in peril.* | Formal/Literary | in peril, put something in peril, grave peril | Useful for strongly emphasizing danger | **Similar:** danger, risk, hazard, threat. **Use peril** for serious danger, often when survival, security, existence, or a highly important outcome is threatened. It has stronger and somewhat literary/formal force: *species in peril*. **Danger** is the general term. **Risk** is the probability or possibility of harm rather than the dangerous state itself. **Hazard** is a source of potential harm, often physical/technical. **Threat** is something capable of causing harm. Thus smoking is a *health hazard/risk*, while someone's life can be *in peril*. |
| **caveat** | /ˈkæviæt/ | A warning, limitation, or condition attached to a statement or proposal | *The policy could be effective, with the important caveat that adequate funding is required.* | Formal | important caveat, major caveat, with the caveat that | Excellent for nuanced arguments | **Similar:** warning, qualification, proviso, condition, limitation. **Use caveat** when accepting or asserting something **while attaching an important limitation, exception, or warning that constrains the claim**: *The method works, with one caveat...* **Warning** primarily alerts someone to danger. **Condition** specifies something that must happen before another thing applies. **Qualification** narrows a statement's validity and is often the closest academic alternative. **Proviso** is a formally stipulated condition, especially legal/contractual. |
| **astute** | /əˈstjuːt/ | Quick to understand situations and make intelligent judgments | *She proved to be an astute business leader.* | Formal | astute observer, astute analysis, astute decision | Excellent for describing judgment or intelligence | **Similar:** perceptive, shrewd, intelligent, insightful, discerning. **Use astute** when someone accurately understands a situation **and uses that understanding to make effective judgments**, especially in business, politics, strategy, or social situations. **Intelligent** refers to general intellectual ability. **Perceptive** emphasizes noticing and understanding subtle things. **Insightful** describes ideas/observations that reveal deep understanding. **Shrewd** adds practical cleverness and sometimes self-interest or calculation. Someone may be mathematically *intelligent* without being an *astute negotiator*. |
| **transpire** | /trænˈspaɪə/ | To happen, or to become known later | *It later transpired that officials had been aware of the problem.* | Formal | it transpired that, later transpired, transpire during | Useful for formal narratives and explanations | **Similar:** happen, occur, emerge, become apparent, come to light. **In modern formal English, use transpire most naturally in “it transpired that...” meaning information later became known.** *It transpired that the data had been falsified.* **Happen/occur** should normally be preferred when you simply mean an event took place. **Emerge/become apparent/come to light** are alternatives when facts become known. Although dictionaries also record *transpire = happen*, using it merely as a sophisticated substitute for *occur* can sound affected. |
| **scapegoat** | /ˈskeɪpɡəʊt/ | A person or group unfairly blamed for a problem or failure | *Immigrants are sometimes used as scapegoats for wider economic problems.* | Neutral/Formal | make someone a scapegoat, scapegoat for failure, political scapegoat | Useful in social and political essays | **Similar:** fall guy, target of blame, culprit, whipping boy. **Use scapegoat** only when the person/group receives **disproportionate or unjust blame**, often so that the genuinely responsible parties avoid responsibility. **Culprit** means the person/entity actually responsible, so it can be the semantic opposite of scapegoat. **Fall guy** is informal and often describes someone deliberately made to take blame. **Target of criticism/blame** does not itself imply that the criticism is unfair. |
| **recount** | /rɪˈkaʊnt/ | To describe or narrate an event or experience in detail | *He recounted his experiences during the crisis.* | Formal | recount a story, recount events, recount experiences | Useful mainly in Speaking and narrative contexts | **Similar:** narrate, relate, describe, retell, recall. **Use recount** when someone tells the sequence/details of something that happened, usually from memory or an existing account. **Narrate** emphasizes telling a story or sequence and can be fictional. **Describe** need not involve events or chronology. **Retell** means tell a story/account again. **Recall** primarily means remember; you can *recall an incident* without recounting it aloud. |
| **pander to** | /ˈpændə tə/ | To satisfy someone's desires or opinions, often for selfish reasons and despite disapproving of them | *Some politicians pander to public fears to gain support.* | Neutral/Negative | pander to voters, pander to public opinion, pander to desires | Strong for media and politics topics | **Similar:** cater to, indulge, appease, accommodate, appeal to. **Use pander to** when someone gratifies people's desires, prejudices, fears, or low tastes **for advantage or popularity**, with clear disapproval: *pander to voters' fears*. **Cater to** is usually neutral and simply means provide what a group wants or needs. **Indulge** means allow excessive desires, often because you are lenient. **Appeal to** means attract or resonate with people and carries no inherent criticism. **Appease** means satisfy demands in order to prevent conflict. |
| **job prospects** | /ˈdʒɒb ˌprɒspekts/ | The likelihood of finding suitable or desirable employment | *A university degree can significantly improve a graduate's job prospects.* | Neutral | improve job prospects, employment prospects, career prospects | Extremely useful in education/employment essays | **Similar:** employment prospects, career prospects, job opportunities, employability. **Use job prospects** for someone's **future likelihood of obtaining good employment**. **Job opportunities** are the actual available vacancies/options in the labour market. **Employability** is the person's capacity/qualities that make them employable. **Career prospects** is broader and includes promotion and long-term advancement after employment. Thus a recession may reduce *job opportunities/prospects*, while acquiring technical skills improves *employability*. |
| **be indebted to** | /bi ɪnˈdetɪd tə/ | To feel deeply grateful to someone for their help or influence | *I am deeply indebted to my mentors for their guidance.* | Formal | deeply indebted to, forever indebted to, indebted to someone | Useful in formal gratitude; less common in Task 2 | **Similar:** be grateful to, owe something to, be obliged to. **Use be indebted to** for **strong, often lasting gratitude** because someone's help or influence was important: *I am indebted to my supervisor*. **Grateful** covers ordinary gratitude and is much more versatile. **Owe X to Y** is excellent when identifying causal contribution: *I owe my success to my teachers*. **Obliged** can mean grateful but can also mean required, so context matters. *Indebted* can literally mean owing money, but *deeply/forever indebted* normally signals gratitude. |
| **deterrent** | /dɪˈterənt/ | Something that discourages someone from doing something | *Tougher sentences may act as a deterrent to serious crime.* | Formal | act as a deterrent, deterrent effect, effective deterrent | Excellent for crime and policy essays | **Similar:** disincentive, preventive measure, obstacle, discouragement. **Use deterrent** when something discourages behaviour through anticipated negative consequences, difficulty, fear, or cost: *fines act as a deterrent*. **Disincentive** is usually an economic/motivational factor reducing the attractiveness of an action. **Preventive measure** actually attempts to stop an event, whereas a deterrent works mainly by influencing behaviour before it occurs. A locked door may be both; punishment is typically described as a deterrent. |
| **emulate** | /ˈemjuleɪt/ | To imitate someone or something with the aim of matching or achieving similar success | *Many young people aspire to emulate the success of prominent athletes and entertainers.* | Formal | emulate success, emulate a model, seek to emulate, aspire to emulate | Strong for development, education, role-model, and celebrity topics | **Similar:** imitate, copy, mimic, model oneself on, follow. **Use emulate** when someone deliberately follows an admired person's/model's behaviour or methods **with the goal of achieving comparable excellence or success**. This positive aspirational component is crucial. **Imitate** simply means reproduce behaviour/style and can be neutral or negative. **Copy** often means reproduce directly and may imply lack of originality. **Mimic** focuses on mannerisms, speech, movements, etc., sometimes humorously. A teenager may *imitate a celebrity's hairstyle* but *emulate the celebrity's professional success or work ethic*. |
| **gauge** | /ɡeɪdʒ/ | To measure, estimate, or assess something | *A survey was conducted to gauge public attitudes towards the proposal.* | Neutral/Formal | gauge reaction, gauge public opinion, gauge progress | Very useful in analytical contexts | **Similar:** measure, assess, estimate, evaluate. **Use gauge** when obtaining an approximate or informed sense of the **level, extent, reaction, attitude, mood, progress, or intensity** of something: *gauge public opinion*. **Measure** is preferable for precise quantitative values. **Assess** means systematically judge condition/quality/significance. **Evaluate** often involves judging merit or effectiveness against criteria. **Estimate** gives an approximate numerical amount. You *measure temperature*, but typically *gauge public sentiment*. |
| **stand up to scrutiny** | /stænd ʌp tə ˈskruːtəni/ | To remain convincing or valid after careful examination | *Her argument does not stand up to scrutiny.* | Formal | stand up to scrutiny, withstand scrutiny, close scrutiny | Excellent for critical evaluation | **Similar:** withstand scrutiny, hold up under examination, be defensible, survive review. **Use stand up to scrutiny** when a claim, argument, theory, explanation, evidence, or policy remains credible after **careful critical examination**. **Withstand scrutiny** is slightly more formal and nearly equivalent. **Survive review** is suitable for formal procedures but does not necessarily imply intense criticism. **Be defensible** means capable of being justified, even if nobody has actually examined it. Do not use *scrutiny* merely for disagreement: an argument can be controversial yet still stand up to scrutiny. |
| **incentive** | /ɪnˈsentɪv/ | Something that motivates or encourages someone to take a particular action | *Tax reductions may provide businesses with an incentive to invest.* | Formal/Neutral | financial incentive, strong incentive, provide an incentive | Extremely common in economics and policy essays | **Similar:** motivation, reward, inducement, encouragement. **Use incentive** for an external or structured factor that makes a particular action more attractive: money, tax relief, promotion, benefits, etc. **Motivation** can be internal: ambition, interest, personal values. **Reward** is something received for behaviour/performance and may function as an incentive, but only if it influences behaviour prospectively. **Inducement** is more formal and can imply deliberate persuasion, occasionally with a negative/bribery-like undertone. |
| **counteract** | /ˌkaʊntərˈækt/ | To reduce, neutralize, or prevent the harmful effect of something | *Governments can introduce targeted policies to counteract rising inequality.* | Formal | counteract effects, counteract influence, counteract negative consequences | Very useful in problem-solution essays | **Similar:** offset, mitigate, neutralize, combat, prevent. **Use counteract** when one force/action works **against an existing opposing effect or tendency**, reducing or neutralizing it: exercise may *counteract the effects of inactivity*. **Prevent** means stop something from occurring in the first place. **Mitigate** means reduce severity without necessarily opposing its cause. **Offset** means compensate for an effect, often quantitatively: emissions may be *offset*. **Combat** means actively fight a problem and is broader. |
| **enviable** | /ˈenviəbl/ | Desirable enough to make other people wish they had the same advantage | *The country is in the enviable position of having abundant renewable-energy resources.* | Formal/Neutral | enviable position, enviable reputation, enviable lifestyle | Good for describing strong advantages | **Similar:** desirable, advantageous, fortunate, privileged. **Use enviable** when a person's/entity's position, reputation, record, lifestyle, etc. is **so favorable that others could reasonably envy it**. **Desirable** simply means worth wanting and does not involve comparison with another possessor. **Advantageous** means providing a practical benefit. **Fortunate** emphasizes luck or favourable circumstances. **Privileged** suggests special advantage/status and may carry social criticism. An *enviable reputation* is natural; an *enviable policy proposal* usually is not unless you mean others wish they possessed it. |
| **legible** | /ˈledʒəbl/ | Clear enough to be read easily | *Students should ensure that their handwriting is legible.* | Neutral | legible handwriting, clearly legible, barely legible | Limited but useful in education contexts | **Similar:** readable, clear, decipherable. **Use legible** specifically when **written/printed characters can physically be recognized**: handwriting, signatures, labels, text. **Readable** is broader: text may be perfectly legible yet not readable because the prose is complicated; *readable* can mean enjoyable/easy to understand. **Decipherable** means possible to work out despite difficulty and therefore is weaker than legible: *barely decipherable handwriting*. **Clear** can refer to meaning, sound, visibility, reasoning, etc., not specifically written characters. |
| **endorse** | /ɪnˈdɔːs/ | To publicly or officially express support or approval | *The council is expected to endorse the recommendations.* | Formal | endorse a policy, endorse a proposal, publicly endorse | Strong for politics, policy, and business | **Similar:** support, approve, recommend, advocate, back. **Use endorse** when a person, institution, or authority **formally or publicly declares approval/support** for a candidate, policy, recommendation, product, etc. **Support** does not need to be public or official. **Approve** may mean formally authorize something, which endorsement alone does not necessarily do. **Recommend** specifically advises others to choose/do something. **Advocate** means actively argue in favour of a position, usually more strongly than endorse. |
| **portrayal** | /pɔːˈtreɪəl/ | The way someone or something is represented or depicted | *The media's portrayal of young people can influence public attitudes towards them.* | Formal | media portrayal, accurate portrayal, negative portrayal | Excellent for media and culture essays | **Similar:** depiction, representation, characterization, image. **Use portrayal** when discussing **how a person, group, event, lifestyle, etc. is presented**, particularly by media, art, literature, film, or discourse; it often invites judgment about accuracy or bias. **Depiction** emphasizes the actual representation, especially visual/artistic. **Representation** is broader and more abstract, including political representation. **Characterization** usually concerns how a person's character is constructed in literature/media. |
| **lucrative** | /ˈluːkrətɪv/ | Producing a large amount of money or profit | *Social-media influencing has become a lucrative career for some young people.* | Formal/Neutral | lucrative business, lucrative career, lucrative market | Excellent for economics, employment, and business topics | **Similar:** profitable, high-paying, remunerative, money-making. **Use lucrative** when an activity, market, career, contract, opportunity, or business generates **substantial financial returns**, often conspicuously so. **Profitable** is technically more precise for businesses/activities whose revenue exceeds costs and does not necessarily mean *highly* profitable. **High-paying** concerns wages/salary received by an individual, not business profit. Thus medicine may be a *lucrative career* or *high-paying profession*, whereas a company can be *profitable* but not *high-paying*. |
| **expenditure** | /ɪkˈspendɪtʃə/ | The amount of money spent on something, especially by a government or organization | *Government expenditure on healthcare has increased considerably.* | Formal | government expenditure, public expenditure, capital expenditure | Extremely useful in Task 1 and economic/policy essays | **Similar:** spending, expense, cost, outlay. **Use expenditure** for formal/economic discussion of **money spent**, particularly by governments, institutions, companies, or sectors: *public expenditure on education*. **Spending** means essentially the same thing but is more general and less formal. **Expense** often refers to a particular cost incurred by a person/business. **Cost** is the amount required to obtain/produce something, not necessarily actual spending. **Outlay** emphasizes an amount paid, often a significant initial investment. |
| **tangible** | /ˈtændʒəbl/ | Real, definite, or capable of being clearly seen, measured, experienced, or demonstrated | *The policy has yet to produce any tangible benefits for low-income households.* | Formal/Neutral | tangible benefits, tangible evidence, tangible results, tangible improvement | Excellent for arguments requiring concrete evidence or measurable outcomes | **Similar:** concrete, measurable, material, physical, discernible. **Use tangible** when something abstract nevertheless has **clear, demonstrable, observable, or practically real existence/effects**: *tangible benefits, progress, evidence, results*. It can also literally mean touchable. **Concrete** contrasts with abstract/vague and emphasizes specificity: *concrete proposal*. **Measurable** specifically means quantifiable; something can be tangible without being numerically measurable. **Material** often means physically existing or significant in practical/legal terms. **Physical** should be used when literal material embodiment is the point. |
| **repercussion** | /ˌriːpəˈkʌʃən/ | An indirect consequence of an action or event, especially a negative one | *The economic repercussions of prolonged unemployment can affect entire communities.* | Formal | serious repercussions, economic repercussions, long-term repercussions, face repercussions | Excellent for cause-and-effect and consequence essays | **Similar:** consequence, effect, ramification, aftermath, implication. **Use repercussion**, usually plural **repercussions**, for **indirect or subsequent negative consequences**, often serious and spreading beyond the immediate event. **Consequence** is neutral regarding positivity/negativity and is much broader. **Effect** is the most general causal result. **Ramification** emphasizes a complex secondary consequence, not necessarily negative. **Aftermath** refers to the situation/period following a destructive or unpleasant event, not one specific consequence. **Implication** can mean a possible logical consequence rather than an outcome that has actually occurred. |
| **perfunctory** | /pəˈfʌŋktəri/ | Done with little effort, interest, or care, usually merely because it is required | *The authorities conducted only a perfunctory investigation into the complaints.* | Formal | perfunctory examination, perfunctory investigation, perfunctory apology, perfunctory glance | Advanced but relatively niche; useful for criticizing inadequate effort or superficial action | **Similar:** cursory, superficial, half-hearted, token, mechanical, reluctant. **Use perfunctory** when an expected/required action **is actually performed but with minimal attention, care, enthusiasm, or sincerity—as though merely fulfilling an obligation**: *a perfunctory apology, inspection, greeting*. **Cursory** emphasizes speed and lack of thoroughness, not necessarily lack of interest: a hurried doctor may perform a *cursory examination* despite caring. **Superficial** emphasizes lack of depth. **Half-hearted** emphasizes weak enthusiasm/commitment and is less formal. **Token** means largely symbolic, done to create the appearance of action: *token consultation*. **Mechanical** emphasizes automatic/routine performance without thought. **Reluctant is NOT a synonym:** it means unwilling or hesitant. Someone can reluctantly conduct a very thorough investigation, or willingly conduct a perfunctory one. |
| **multitude** | /ˈmʌltɪtjuːd/ | A very large number of people, things, problems, or possibilities | *Urbanisation can create a multitude of social and environmental challenges.* | Formal/Neutral | a multitude of problems, a multitude of factors, a multitude of reasons, a multitude of opportunities | Very useful for introducing multiple causes, factors, problems, or benefits | **Similar:** many, numerous, myriad, a large number of, variety. **Use a multitude of** to emphasize a **very large number of countable entities**, especially factors, problems, possibilities, people, etc. It is stronger and more rhetorical than *many*. **Numerous** is formal and restrained. **A large number of** is safest when quantity alone matters. **Myriad** similarly suggests an extremely large number and is more literary/formal. **A variety of** is importantly different: it emphasizes **diversity of types**, not quantity. Ten very different solutions may constitute *a variety of solutions* without being *a multitude*. |
