# Tips from Codex

a **large repertoire of conventional Task 1 phrases tied to visual patterns**.

The key is to learn each phrase as:

**phrase → precise meaning → what the graph should look like → example**

That prevents exactly the kind of problem you have had with expressions such as *rose stably* or *stable decrease*: the intended idea is sensible, but the phrase does not correspond to a conventional statistical description.

I would start with quantitative Task 1 because that is where the largest phrase bank is useful.

## 1. Overall direction and trend shape

| Phrase | What it means | What the graph should look like | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **rose steadily** | Increased at a fairly consistent rate | Mostly continuous upward movement, without major jumps or reversals | *The proportion of two-car households rose steadily from 7% to 26%.* |
| **fell steadily** | Decreased at a fairly consistent rate | Mostly continuous downward movement | *The percentage of households without a car fell steadily over the period.* |
| **increased gradually** | Increased slowly over time | Gentle upward slope | *Seafood consumption increased gradually from 18 to 22 kg.* |
| **declined gradually** | Decreased slowly | Gentle downward slope | *The unemployment rate declined gradually after 2010.* |
| **rose sharply** | Increased substantially over a relatively short period | Steep upward section | *Sales rose sharply between 2005 and 2007.* |
| **fell sharply** | Large, relatively rapid decrease | Steep downward section | *Meat consumption fell sharply after 1990.* |
| **increased considerably** | Large overall increase; speed is not necessarily implied | End substantially above start | *The proportion of two-car households increased considerably.* |
| **declined substantially** | Large overall decrease | End substantially below start | *The amount of forest declined substantially.* |
| **showed an upward trend** | General direction was upward | May contain some fluctuations but overall rise | *Fruit consumption showed an upward trend throughout the period.* |
| **showed a downward trend** | General direction was downward | May contain fluctuations, but overall fall | *The percentage without a car showed a downward trend.* |
| **followed an upward trend** | Same basic meaning as above | Overall upward trajectory | *Exports followed a broadly upward trend.* |
| **followed a downward trend** | Overall trajectory downward | Some local movements allowed | *Beef consumption followed a downward trend after 1980.* |
| **was broadly increasing** | Generally increased despite irregularities | Overall upward, but not smoothly | *Demand was broadly increasing despite several short-term declines.* |
| **was broadly declining** | Generally decreased despite temporary rises or plateaus | Start high, end lower, with small interruptions | *The proportion of households without a car was broadly declining throughout the period.* |

### “Broadly declining” in particular

This is useful when **“fell steadily” would be too strong**.

Suppose the values are:

> 48 → 45 → 44 → 42 → 43 → 39 → 36 → 34 → 31 → 32 → 30 → 28 → 25
> 

The line clearly moves downward overall, but it occasionally rises.

So:

> **fell steadily** ⚠️ slightly inaccurate because there are reversals
> 
> 
> **was broadly declining** ✅
> 
> **followed a broadly downward trend** ✅
> 
> **declined overall** ✅
> 

**Broadly** means *when viewed as a whole / in general*, not *at every individual point*.

This is an extremely useful word in Task 1.

## 2. When the trend is not perfectly smooth

| Phrase | Meaning | Visual pattern | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **fluctuated slightly** | Small repeated rises and falls | Small zigzags around approximately the same level | *The figure for one-car households fluctuated slightly around 44%.* |
| **fluctuated considerably** | Large repeated changes | Pronounced peaks and troughs | *Vegetable consumption fluctuated considerably over the period.* |
| **fluctuated between X and Y** | Values repeatedly remained within a range | Repeated movement inside clear upper/lower bounds | *The figure fluctuated between 40% and 45%.* |
| **remained relatively stable** | Changed only a little overall | Almost flat line, perhaps with minor movement | *Seafood consumption remained relatively stable.* |
| **remained broadly stable** | Generally stable despite some noticeable small changes | No meaningful long-term direction | *One-car ownership remained broadly stable at around 44%.* |
| **showed little change** | Start and end or overall values are very similar | Almost flat / limited net change | *The proportion showed little change over the 36-year period.* |
| **changed only marginally** | Very small change | Tiny difference between relevant values | *The figure changed only marginally, from 43% to 44%.* |
| **remained virtually unchanged** | Almost no change whatsoever | Practically flat | *The percentage remained virtually unchanged at approximately 10%.* |
| **levelled off** | Was changing, then became stable | Rise/fall followed by a flat section | *After reaching 30%, the figure levelled off.* |
| **stabilised at X** | Eventually settled around a particular value | Movement first, then flat/stable | *The rate declined before stabilising at around 5%.* |
| **plateaued at X** | Reached a level and stayed there | Clearly flat after earlier movement | *Sales plateaued at approximately 80 million units.* |

A distinction that caused trouble in your previous essay:

> **remained stable** = it was already stable
> 
> 
> **levelled off** = it was changing **and then** became stable
> 

So if the sequence is:

> 1 → 1 → 1 → 1
> 

say:

> **remained stable at 1%**
> 

If it is:

> 1 → 3 → 6 → 10 → 10 → 10
> 

say:

> **rose to 10% and then levelled off**
>

## 3. Temporary reversals

These are especially useful for line graphs.

| Phrase | What happens | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **dipped slightly** | Small temporary decrease | *The figure dipped slightly in 1998 before recovering.* |
| **fell briefly** | Short-lived decline | *Consumption fell briefly in the mid-1980s.* |
| **temporarily declined** | Decline interrupted a broader upward trend | *Fruit consumption temporarily declined in the 1970s.* |
| **recovered** | Rose after having fallen | *After dropping to 80 kg, the figure recovered to 100 kg.* |
| **rebounded** | Relatively strong recovery after a decline | *Sales rebounded sharply the following year.* |
| **resumed its upward trend** | Was rising, temporarily stopped/fell, then started rising again | *After a brief decline, the figure resumed its upward trend.* |
| **resumed its decline** | Was falling, temporarily stabilised/rose, then fell again | *The rate stabilised briefly before resuming its decline.* |
| **reversed direction** | Changed from rising to falling or vice versa | *The trend reversed after 2005.* |
| **turned downward** | Started falling after previously rising/stabilising | *After peaking in 2010, the figure turned downward.* |
| **turned upward** | Started rising after previously falling | *The rate turned upward in the final years.* |

I would use **rebounded** more cautiously than **recovered** because *rebound* suggests a relatively noticeable recovery.

## 4. Peaks, lows and turning points

| Phrase | Exact use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **reached a peak of X** | Reached its maximum and normally fell afterwards | *Meat consumption reached a peak of nearly 120 kg in 1990.* |
| **peaked at X** | Concise equivalent | *The figure peaked at 65% in 2005.* |
| **reached its highest level at X** | Safe alternative | *Exports reached their highest level at $8 billion.* |
| **reached a low of X** | Reached the minimum | *Consumption reached a low of 60 kg.* |
| **fell to a low of X** | Falling trend reached minimum | *The rate fell to a low of 4%.* |
| **bottomed out at X** | Fell to lowest point, usually before recovery | *Sales bottomed out at 20 million before recovering.* |
| **hit X** | Reached a figure; acceptable but slightly less formal | *The rate hit 10% in 2012.* |
| **reached X** | Completely neutral | *The proportion reached 26% in 2007.* |

For IELTS, **reached** is often all you need.

You previously used *reached a high of 26%* merely because 26% was the final and highest value. That's not wrong, but:

> **rose to 26%**
> 

is usually better if there is no particular analytical reason to emphasize the maximum.

Use **peak** when the shape matters:

> rise → maximum → fall
>

## 5. Size of change versus speed of change

This distinction is important.

### Magnitude: how much did it change?

| Small | Moderate | Large |
| --- | --- | --- |
| slightly | moderately | considerably |
| marginally | noticeably | substantially |
| modestly | appreciably | significantly |
| a slight increase | a moderate increase | a substantial increase |
| a small decline | a noticeable decline | a considerable decline |

### Speed: how quickly did it happen?

| Slow | Fast |
| --- | --- |
| gradually | rapidly |
| slowly | sharply |
| steadily* | quickly |
| progressively | abruptly |

**Steadily** is special: it mainly means **consistently**, not necessarily slowly.

A line can:

> **rise steadily and rapidly**
> 

if it rises steeply at a consistent rate.

Similarly:

> **a substantial gradual increase**
> 

can describe a very large increase that occurs slowly over 30 years.

This is why phrases such as your earlier **“significant but stable decrease”** were problematic. What you wanted was probably:

> **a substantial but gradual decline**
> 

where:

- **substantial** describes magnitude;
- **gradual** describes speed.

That pairing is perfectly logical.

## 6. Starting and ending values

These are intentionally simple.

| Function | Natural options |
| --- | --- |
| Starting | **stood at X**, **began at X**, **started at X**, **was X in 1990** |
| Ending | **ended at X**, **finished at X**, **stood at X by 2010**, **reached X by 2010** |
| Change start → finish | **rose from X to Y**, **fell from X to Y**, **increased from X to Y**, **declined from X to Y** |

Examples:

> **The figure stood at approximately 48% in 1971.**
> 

> **It fell to around 25% by 2007.**
> 

> **Two-car ownership rose from approximately 7% to 26%.**
> 

Be cautious with:

> **initially constituted 7%**
> 

because *constituted* needs something that is being constituted:

> **Two-car households constituted 7% of all households.**
> 

Whereas:

> **The figure stood at 7%.**
> 

requires much less grammatical machinery.

## 7. Comparing two series

| Phrase | When to use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **whereas** | Direct contrast | *Fruit consumption increased, whereas meat consumption declined.* |
| **while** | Contrast or simultaneous trends | *The no-car figure fell, while two-car ownership rose.* |
| **by contrast** | Start a contrasting sentence | *By contrast, seafood consumption changed very little.* |
| **in contrast** | Same function | *In contrast, the proportion with one car remained stable.* |
| **compared with** | Direct numerical comparison | *The figure was 35%, compared with 20% in regional areas.* |
| **the corresponding figure for X was...** | Compare same measure/category elsewhere | *The corresponding figure for regional centres was 27%.* |
| **respectively** | Match two or more items with numbers in the same order | *The figures for cities and regional centres were 35% and 27%, respectively.* |
| **was higher/lower than** | Straight comparison | *The figure for cities was higher than that for regional centres.* |
| **exceeded X by...** | Numerically surpassed | *City income exceeded the regional figure by 8 percentage points.* |
| **was twice as high as** | Approximately 2× | *The figure was roughly twice as high as that for seafood.* |
| **was similar to** | Values reasonably close | *Rice exports were similar to seafood exports.* |
| **was almost identical to** | Extremely close | *Rice and seafood recorded almost identical values.* |

A very useful construction:

> **X rose, whereas Y declined.**
> 

It is simple, but that is exactly why it works.

## 8. Crossovers and changing rank

These are often excellent “main features.”

| Phrase | Pattern | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **overtook X** | Y starts below X and later becomes higher | *Two-car households eventually overtook households without a car.* |
| **surpassed X** | Became greater than X | *Fruit consumption surpassed meat consumption in the 1990s.* |
| **exceeded X** | Was greater numerically | *Exports exceeded imports after 2015.* |
| **remained above/below X** | Never crossed | *Vegetable consumption remained above that of fruit throughout.* |
| **was the largest category throughout** | Ranked first for whole period | *Vegetables remained the most widely consumed category throughout.* |
| **became the largest category** | Ranking changed | *One-car households became the largest group in the late 1970s.* |
| **fell to second place** | Lost rank | *The no-car category subsequently fell to second place.* |
| **remained the smallest category** | Consistently last | *Households with three or more cars remained the smallest group.* |

**Overtake** is particularly useful because it compresses an entire crossover into one verb.

## 9. Approximation

You asked about **roughly** earlier. These are all useful but not identical.

| Phrase | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **approximately 30%** | close to 30 |
| **around 30%** | close to 30 |
| **roughly 30%** | close to 30 |
| **about 30%** | close to 30 |
| **nearly 30%** | below but close to 30 |
| **almost 30%** | below but close to 30 |
| **just under 30%** | slightly below 30 |
| **just over 30%** | slightly above 30 |
| **slightly more than 30%** | somewhat above 30 |
| **slightly less than 30%** | somewhat below 30 |

The first four are approximate without specifying direction.

Do not write:

> *approximately just under 30%*
> 

because you are stacking approximation unnecessarily.

## 10. Shares, percentages and categories

These are useful for pie charts, bar charts and static comparisons.

| Expression | Natural grammar | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **accounted for** | X accounted for Y% of total | *Coffee accounted for 35% of exports.* |
| **made up** | X made up Y% | *Middle-income households made up 35% of the population.* |
| **constituted** | X constituted Y% of total | *The category constituted approximately one third of the total.* |
| **represented** | X represented Y% | *Cars represented the smallest category.* |
| **comprised** | Whole comprised components | *The total comprised six categories.* |
| **the proportion/share of** | flexible noun phrase | *The share of high-income residents was 20%.* |

Be careful with **comprise**:

> **The population comprised four income groups.** ✅
> 
> 
> **Four groups comprised the population.** ⚠️ traditionally disputed / often avoided in careful formal prose
> 
> **Coffee comprised 23 million dollars.** ❌
> 

For your purposes, **accounted for / represented / made up** are easier.

## 11. Absolute quantities and monetary values

This matters because you previously tried to use *make up* with dollar values.

If the graph gives **$23 million**, rather than **23%**, use:

| Phrase | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **was worth** | *Coffee exports were worth $23 million.* |
| **was valued at** | *Machinery exports were valued at $30.5 million.* |
| **stood at** | *The value of exports stood at $23 million.* |
| **reached** | *Export earnings reached $40 million.* |
| **recorded a value of** | *The category recorded a value of $16 million.* |

For percentages:

> **Coffee accounted for 35% of exports.**
> 

For monetary values:

> **Coffee exports were worth $23 million.**
> 

That distinction is worth making automatic.

## 12. Grouping several categories without listing everything

This is especially useful for the selective style you prefer.

| Phrase | Use |
| --- | --- |
| **The remaining categories were considerably smaller.** | Avoid narrating every small category |
| **The two categories recorded similar figures.** | Group similar values |
| **X and Y followed broadly similar patterns.** | Similar trends |
| **X and Y moved in opposite directions.** | One rises, one falls |
| **The largest increases were seen in X and Y.** | Group biggest changes |
| **By comparison, the other categories changed relatively little.** | Contrast dynamic vs stable categories |
| **The figures for X, Y and Z were all below 10%.** | Compress three small categories |
| **X remained well ahead of the other categories.** | Persistent dominant category |

Example:

Instead of:

> *Seafood was 4.4 million. Rice was 4.3 million. Garments were 2 million. Other was 2 million.*
> 

write:

> **The remaining categories were considerably smaller, with seafood and rice both worth just over $4 million and garments and other goods valued at $2 million each.**
> 

That's exactly the kind of **compression** that improves Task 1.

## 13. Useful “overall” descriptions

These are especially useful for overviews.

| Visual pattern | Natural overview wording |
| --- | --- |
| Most categories rise | **Most categories increased over the period.** |
| Everything rises except one | **All categories increased except X, which declined.** |
| Two rise, two fall | **X and Y increased, whereas A and B declined.** |
| One stable | **X remained largely unchanged throughout.** |
| Large divergence | **The gap between X and Y widened considerably.** |
| Convergence | **The figures for X and Y became increasingly similar.** |
| Persistent leader | **X remained the largest category throughout the period.** |
| Ranking changes | **X overtook Y to become the largest category.** |
| Mixed movements, clear overall direction | **Despite some fluctuations, X showed an overall upward trend.** |
| No dominant trend | **The figures fluctuated throughout the period, with no clear overall trend.** |

Two words I particularly want you to internalise are:

**overall** and **broadly**.

They let you describe the **macro-pattern without falsely claiming that every individual movement follows it**.

For example:

> **Despite several fluctuations, vegetable consumption increased overall.**
> 

> **The figure followed a broadly downward trend.**
> 

These are safer than forcing **steadily** onto an irregular line.

## 14. Map language

Your vocabulary requirements become completely different for maps.

| Function | Useful language | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Existing location | **was located / was situated** | *A house was situated to the west of the shop.* |
| Land coverage | **occupied** | *Farmland occupied much of the southern area.* |
| Replacement | **was replaced by** | *Part of the forest was replaced by an airport.* |
| Conversion | **was converted into** | *Farmland was converted into residential land.* |
| New construction | **was built / constructed** | *A school was constructed in the centre.* |
| Expansion | **was expanded / extended** | *The road network was considerably expanded.* |
| Reduction | **was reduced / shrank considerably** | *The forested area shrank considerably.* |
| Retention | **was retained / remained** | *A small area of forest was retained.* |
| No change | **remained unchanged** | *The western road remained unchanged.* |
| Position | **to the north/east/south/west of** | *The airport was built to the east of the apartments.* |
| Proximity | **adjacent to / next to** | *A shop was constructed adjacent to the school.* |
| Corner | **in the north-eastern corner** | *The airport occupies the north-eastern corner.* |

Again, notice how ordinary the verbs are.

**Built**, **replaced**, **expanded**, **remained** will almost always outperform creative alternatives such as *introduced*, *wrapped around*, or *built on top of*.

## 15. Process language

For process diagrams, you mainly need sequence and transformation.

| Function | Useful language | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Start | **The process begins with...** | *The process begins with a list of requirements being drawn up.* |
| Next | **This is followed by...** | *This is followed by the publication of the job listing.* |
| Next | **After this / Subsequently** | *Subsequently, the applications are assessed.* |
| Sequence | **Once X has been completed,...** | *Once the form has been completed, it is submitted.* |
| Branch | **Those who... whereas those who...** | *Those who are unsuccessful are rejected, whereas suitable candidates proceed to interview.* |
| Conditional | **If..., then...** | *If the application is successful, the candidate is invited to interview.* |
| Final | **The process concludes with...** | *The process concludes with the successful candidate being offered the job.* |
| Final | **Finally,...** | *Finally, the selected applicant receives a job offer.* |

You do **not** need twenty synonyms for *then*.

Processes often become clearer when the cohesion comes from grammatical relationships:

> **Once the applications have been assessed, unsuccessful candidates are notified by email, whereas suitable applicants proceed to an interview.**
> 

rather than:

> *Firstly... secondly... subsequently... furthermore... thereafter...*
>

# What I would actually memorise

I wouldn't try to memorize everything above equally.

For your own writing, I would first make about **30–40 expressions automatic**. Particularly:

**Direction:**

*rose/fell steadily, increased gradually, rose/fell sharply, increased considerably, declined substantially, broadly upward/downward, increased overall*

**Stability:**

*remained relatively stable, showed little change, fluctuated slightly, levelled off, stabilised at*

**Values:**

*stood at, rose from X to Y, fell to, reached, peaked at, just over/under, approximately, roughly*

**Comparison:**

*whereas, while, compared with, the corresponding figure, remained above, overtook, was the largest/smallest*

**Shares:**

*accounted for, represented, made up*

**Maps:**

*occupied, was replaced by, was converted into, was constructed, was expanded, remained unchanged*

**Processes:**

*begins with, is followed by, subsequently, once..., whereas..., concludes with*

The goal is for these expressions to become so familiar that when you see a graph, you don't think:

> “What sophisticated verb could I use here?”
> 

You think:

> “This line is irregular but downward → **broadly declining**.”
> 
> 
> “This line rose and then became flat → **levelled off**.”
> 
> “These lines cross → **overtook**.”
> 
> “This category barely moved → **remained relatively stable**.”
> 
> “This forest became an airport → **was replaced by**.”
> 

That kind of **visual pattern → linguistic pattern mapping** is probably the most useful thing you can train for Task 1 right now.
